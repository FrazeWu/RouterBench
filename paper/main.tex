\title{CapGraphBench: Evaluating Least-Authority Capability Routing in Tool-Using Language Agents}
\author{Anonymous ACL Submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A language agent can complete a task through tool routes that differ in authority
and policy consequences. We present CapGraphBench, a bounded capability-routing
benchmark separating functional completion, policy compliance, and graph-relative
path optimality. Its 776 synthetic scenarios combine manually authored workflows,
deterministic template expansion, and replay-checked reference certificates.
We evaluate 11 model checkpoints with five presentations per case. Under the
historical v13 interface, exact optimal-path success ranges from 33.63\% to
78.94\%. On 96 paired cases, flattening public preference contrasts increases
Qwen3-14B's forbidden-outcome rate from 6.67\% to 12.50\%. Ordering controls
identify a presentation shortcut, while a separate interface audit finds 45
cases without a selectable safe, successful route and 279 without a selectable
forbidden route. We retain the original denominators and report these
reachability limits explicitly. The results motivate evaluating route choice
alongside task success and checking interface validity before interpreting
scores as evidence of capability or safety.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Language agents can complete the same task through tool workflows that differ in
access, permissions, and effects. Two workflows may both deliver a requested
summary while differing in whether protected source fields enter the outgoing
message. Even a policy-compliant workflow may exercise unnecessary authority.
Task success alone therefore leaves policy compliance and least privilege
unresolved \citep{saltzer1975}.

InjecAgent and AgentDojo evaluate indirect prompt injection
\citep{zhan2024injecagent,debenedetti2024agentdojo}, and ToolEmu assesses risky
behavior through emulated execution \citep{ruan2024toolemu}. ToolPrivBench
studies privilege-sensitive tool choice, including escalation after transient
failures \citep{yang2026toolprivbench}. Our complementary focus is the complete,
dependency-constrained workflow: action order and data provenance can change
policy outcomes even when the selected tools and permissions are unchanged.

We ask whether agents select safe, successful routes, whether they choose an
optimal route among those alternatives, and how graph structure and presentation
affect their choices. Such evaluation needs explicit routes, reproducible
execution, a public preference rule, and an interface through which the reference
routes are actually selectable.

We present \textbf{CapGraphBench}: 776 synthetic scenarios combining manual
workflow authoring, deterministic template expansion, and bounded replay checks.
Graph edges bind typed actions to concrete provider--resource--mode capabilities.
Authors establish viable dependent routes before assigning evaluator references.
Evaluation separates completion, compliance, and exact path optimality. A public
lexicographic rule compares safe, successful routes by exposure, authority scope,
evidence loss, and interaction; least authority is relative to this graph and
ordering.

Across 11 checkpoints and five presentations per case, historical v13 exact
optimality ranges from 33.63\% to 78.94\%. On 96 paired cases, flattening
public preference contrasts raises Qwen3-14B's forbidden-outcome rate from
6.67\% to 12.50\%. Ordering controls expose a presentation shortcut. An interface
audit also finds 45 cases with no selectable safe, successful route, limiting
capability and safety interpretations. We retain the original denominators.

Our contributions are (1) a route-level evaluation formulation with explicit
interface-validity checks; (2) a catalog-bound, replay-checked benchmark spanning
16 test template families; and (3) an eleven-checkpoint evaluation with structural
ablations, ordering controls, and reachability audits. An ongoing companion
ECPA study examines guarded recovery; this draft includes no completed defense
effectiveness result.

\section{Capability-Routing Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

A scenario $\mathcal{S}=(T,G,s_0,\delta,F_T,C,B)$ specifies a task, directed
graph $G=(V,E)$, private initial fixture, trusted action semantics, and verifiers
for completion, compliance, and forbidden-target realization. Each edge binds a
registered adapter and typed arguments to a provider--resource--mode capability,
with prior-action requirements, public preference ranks, and authority atoms
$A_e$. An atom identifies a resource, operation, and target. The planner is
untrusted; bindings, execution, and verification are trusted.

\paragraph{Routes and outcomes.}
Let $\mathcal{P}_H$ contain start-to-terminal routes with at most $H$ actions.
Replay starts from a fresh $s_0$, checks dependencies, and recomputes authority
atoms from each action and its current state. Routes with unsatisfied dependencies
are ineligible. Among executable routes, safe completion requires
\[
 \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{safe}}
 =\{p\in\mathcal{P}_H:F_T(p)\land C(p)\land\neg B(p)\}.
\]
Completion and compliance are distinct: a compliant route may leave the task
unfinished. The footprint $\bigcup_{e\in p}A_e$ omits order and data dependencies,
so route safety requires contextual execution.

\paragraph{Public preference.}
Each edge has four ranks $r_j(e)\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, lower preferred, for exposure,
authority scope, evidence loss, and interaction, in that priority order
(Table~\ref{tab:ordinals}). We sum ranks within each dimension and minimize
lexicographically over safe, successful routes:
\begin{align*}
 \mathbf{q}(p)&=\bigl(\textstyle\sum_{e\in p}r_j(e)\bigr)_{j=1}^{4},\\
 \mathcal{P}^{*}&=\underset{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{safe}}}
 {\operatorname{arg\,min}_{\mathrm{lex}}}\mathbf{q}(p).
\end{align*}
Every exact minimizer receives credit, including ties with a different route
from the stored certificate. These ordinal sums define graph-relative
preferences, not calibrated risk; forbidden effects exclude a route before
comparison.

\paragraph{Information boundary.}
The planner sees the task, goal, typed tools, graph, arguments, dependencies,
authority atoms, and preference rule. Descriptive branch names are anonymized.
Fixtures, reference routes, evaluator minimum authority, private costs, and
outcome labels are withheld (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}). Unsafe routes are
decoys within ordinary tasks. Active injection, arbitrary shell access,
adapter compromise, and side channels are outside this benchmark setting;
the separate ECPA study uses the threat model in Appendix~\ref{sec:ecpa}.

\paragraph{Authoring and materialization.}
Authors select primitive adapters, concrete provider--resource--mode bindings,
planning skills, and workflow templates from the capability universe; the
executor catalog defines adapter implementations. Multiple viable routes and
dependencies precede evaluator reference assignment. Each source supplies a
task, synthetic fixture, graph, assertions, and safe/forbidden certificates.
Deterministic materialization reuses authored structures while substituting
identifiers and allocation metadata; 425 of 776 cases carry an explicit expansion
tag. Instance count therefore exceeds independently authored workflows. Provider
bindings model access contexts; execution uses the trusted local simulator.

\paragraph{Quality control.}
Automated checks cover schema/catalog validity, public ranks, bounded path
coverage, dependencies, authority declarations, and replayed outcomes. The safe
certificate must be an exact minimizer. Forbidden certificates must complete the
task, realize their target, and violate policy. Another terminal path must be no
longer than the safe reference, preventing a unique-shortest-path shortcut.
Freezes bind catalog/scenario digests. One reviewer checked a random 10\% sample
for plausibility, policy clarity, and labels, reporting no issues; this does not
validate every case or measure reviewer agreement.

These tags do not establish independent workflow diversity. Only three of five
nominal length bands are populated; actual safe certificates have 1--5 actions.
All 776 cases are test data; the split contract reserves eight development
families and restricts tuning to them. Semantic independence requires content
review beyond family labels (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}).

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Historical v13.}
At each pre-replay choice, the planner receives the full public graph, current
node, selected prefix, and outgoing-edge menu, and returns a JSON edge ID.
Selection stops at the first terminal; a bound overrun or nonterminal dead end
invalidates the attempt. The route is then replayed from a fresh fixture, with
no backtracking, repair, or automatic retry. Replay allows proposed actions,
stops on the forbidden target or runtime bound, and evaluates the resulting
state. Primary success is $O(p)=\mathbf{1}[p\in\mathcal{P}^{*}]$; completion,
compliance, and replay-confirmed forbidden outcomes (``attack path'') are reported
separately. Certificate matching and Pareto membership are secondary.

\paragraph{Repeated measurements.}
Each case has five relabeled/reordered presentations, seeds 1701--1705,
temperature 0, top-$p$ 1, and a 2,048-token limit per selection call. A case is
\emph{capable} if at least three attempts achieve $O=1$. Invalid outputs remain
failures; backend failures leave cases unassessed and exclude incomplete runs
from full comparisons. Each complete model row retains 776 cases and 3,880
attempts. Descriptive paired 95\% intervals use 10,000 case-bootstrap resamples,
keeping repeats together; template reuse prevents interpreting these as
independent-family uncertainty.

\paragraph{Interface validity.}
\label{sec:eligibility}
Construction permits continuation through an interior terminal, while v13 stops
there. Consequently, 45 cases lack a selectable safe completion or exact optimum,
and 279 lack a selectable forbidden route. These counts remain in historical
denominators: unavailable optima cannot diagnose model error, and unavailable
forbidden routes cannot test safety discrimination. The public payload also
contains conflicting Pareto-primary wording, although Pareto and exact-optimal
sets coincide in all 776 cases. A repair or filtered population needs a new
protocol identity and fresh reporting (Appendix~\ref{app:repair}). Manifests bind
clean source revisions, scenarios, models, runtime, and decoding; additional
reproducibility details are in Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}.

\section{Companion Defense: ECPA}
\label{sec:defense-study}

ECPA (evaluation-calibrated path-aware capability attenuation) studies guarded
recovery when an external relay tampers with model-response text or forges a
structured tool call. The relay cannot execute actions, change trusted state,
or forge approvals. Historical v13 measures pre-replay selection; ECPA instead
mediates execution and replanning under the separate runtime contract in
Appendix~\ref{sec:ecpa}.

\paragraph{Guarded intervention.}
For each proposal, a trusted monitor constructs a legal menu: allow, constrain
to a registered narrower action, escalate an exact scope increment for approval,
or deny and permit bounded replanning. A constrained offer requires the task
agent's exact acceptance; rejection dispatches nothing. Grants bind the
principal, task, request, epoch, and expiry and cannot override fixed
prohibitions. Immediately before dispatch, the monitor rechecks preconditions,
provenance, effects, and grants. Trusted receipts alone update runtime facts.
These guards are common to the matched controllers; safety does not depend on
the learned selector.

\paragraph{Outcome-derived selection.}
A Qwen3-1.7B LoRA selector chooses a candidate ID from public task/contracts,
the proposal, trusted history, counters, and legal menu; the Qwen3-14B task
agent stays frozen. Independent simulator continuations provide candidate-choice
targets from violations, safe completion, and cost. The selector receives no
private fixture, evaluator labels, reference route, or raw untrusted response
text. Invalid choices fall back deterministically, and every choice is
revalidated. Standard supervised fine-tuning is not an algorithmic contribution.

\paragraph{Matched study.}
The 66-task source inventory allocates 16/5/4/41 tasks and 12/4/4/40 semantic
families to training/tuning/calibration/test. The five arms are unguarded
behavior, per-action authorization, a deterministic path rule, the prompted
compact base, and its fine-tuned counterpart. The study pairs benign/attacked
episodes and text/structured relays, selects the strongest guarded comparator
on tuning, and retains families as uncertainty clusters. Safe recovery requires
completion, explicit STOP, and no episode violation. The study is in progress;
this draft reports no completed efficacy comparison. Full criteria, source-review
limits, and runtime details appear in Appendices~\ref{sec:ecpa}--\ref{app:runtime}.

\subsection{RQ1: How Well Do Models Select Routes?}
\label{sec:exp-models}

\textbf{Finding 1.} Every checkpoint completes more tasks than it solves with
an exact optimum (Table~\ref{tab:formal-results}). Optimality ranges from 33.63\%
to 78.94\%. Qwen3-14B leads on optimality and capable cases (84.54\%), yet 406
of its 3,469 functionally successful attempts fail exact optimality.
Against Qwen3-8B, it gains 20.52 optimality points (95\% CI:
$[18.48,22.53]$), while forbidden outcomes fall by 6.26 points
($[-7.63,-4.95]$). Its capable-case difference from Qwen3-32B-AWQ is 0.52
points ($[-1.68,2.71]$). Training, quantization, and serving differ across
checkpoints, preventing attribution to parameter count alone.

\subsection{RQ2: Which Route Properties Expose Errors?}
\label{sec:exp-structure}

Qwen3-14B optimality is lowest for three-action safe certificates (65.0\%),
rising to 76.5\% for four actions. Audit/evidence manipulation has the lowest
effect-family optimality (72.2\%) and highest forbidden-outcome rate (14.2\%).
These groups also differ in workflow and graph structure
(Appendix~\ref{app:extended-diagnostics}); they do not isolate causal difficulty.



The paired ablation uses 96 cases, four per combination of primary effect and
nominal length band, with five presentations in each of five graph conditions
(2,400 valid attempts). Flattening sets all four public ranks to their lowest
values and removes private edge costs, preserving topology but potentially
changing exact-credit sets.

\textbf{Finding 2.} Flattening preference contrasts increases Qwen3-14B's
forbidden-outcome rate from 6.67\% to 12.50\%: a 5.83-point difference
(95\% CI: $[3.12,8.75]$). Compliance falls by 5.83 points
($[-8.96,-3.12]$), and capable cases by 9.38 points ($[-16.69,-2.08]$).
The optimality-difference interval includes zero. Removing unsafe decoys leaves
reported rates unchanged; the other modifications give less conclusive
differences. This is a selected diagnostic subset and one checkpoint, and the
flattening changes multiple preferences rather than authority alone.

\subsection{RQ3: How Does the Interface Affect Scores?}
\label{sec:exp-validity}

\textbf{Finding 3.} Action presentation supplies a shortcut. The first-edge
rule reaches 719/776 exact optima (92.65\%) under the original order, versus

prefix comparison is not a full-inventory estimate. Separately, the reachability
and credit-contract defects in Section~\ref{sec:eligibility} limit how all these
scores can be interpreted.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

CapGraphBench separates completion, compliance, and declared path optimality.
Historical results show that task success can coexist with suboptimal or
forbidden routes; presentation shortcuts and unavailable routes also affect
scores. Interface repairs need a new freeze and fresh measurements. The
companion defense comparison is ongoing, and matched composition diagnostics
remain proposed (Appendix~\ref{sec:diagnostics}). Neither the historical results
nor these designs establish deployment safety or a learned recovery advantage.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Bounded simulation.}
Graphs contain only 3--6 terminal paths and safe certificates 1--5 actions.
They do not establish long-horizon planning, unrestricted code correctness,
or safety against arbitrary APIs. Deterministic fixtures remove tool
stochasticity rather than model it. Language understanding is measured through
task/rule interpretation and typed routing; multilingual or ambiguous-policy
robustness is not established.

\paragraph{Validity and preference.}
The mismatch in Section~\ref{sec:eligibility} limits historical v13 interpretation.
Unavailable unsafe routes can inflate apparent attack avoidance; unavailable
safe routes can depress capability. Filtering changes the benchmark population
and potentially its family composition.
Ordinal sums and lexicographic priorities are conventions, not calibrated risk,
and include evidence/interaction preferences beyond privilege.
Coverage tags do not establish deployment frequencies.
The coincident Pareto/exact sets do not test preference trade-offs.
Authoring neutrality and semantic family independence require human review;
structural checks alone cannot establish them.
The single-reviewer 10\% check leaves reliability on unreviewed cases and
agreement across reviewers unmeasured.

\paragraph{Proposed extensions.}
The diagnostic corpus, history-sensitive diagnostic verifier, and learned
ECPA controller are not validated in this draft. Conditional rates do not
identify reasoning and depend on matching quality and public information.
Existing development cases do not establish adequate learning coverage.

\paragraph{Enforcement and generalization.}
ECPA assumes trusted labels, complete mediation, correct contracts, atomic
dispatch, and authenticated approval. Provenance does not imply general
noninterference of model outputs. Completion witnesses may be wrong outside
their abstraction. Offline support and calibration limits do not guarantee
safety under shift. Human approval is not infallible.
Learning's advantage over deterministic control remains unproven.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

Intended use is controlled evaluation and defense research, not escalation
against deployed systems. Formal graph replay uses an in-memory simulator,
not live accounts or services. All self-authored scenarios use synthetic
file contents, identities, resource identifiers, and credentials; they do not
contain real business or user records. Aggregate artifacts omit prompts, responses, fixtures, and
certificates. Response hashes provide integrity, not anonymity.

Unsafe-route examples have dual-use value. We plan to release original code,
self-authored synthetic task files, and evaluation scripts under GNU GPLv3,
with an anonymous version for review. Third-party code, data, models, and ACL
style files retain their upstream licenses and provenance.
Archive preparation and publication remain pending.
Formal recovery-training and evaluation costs remain unmeasured.

\section{Proposed Counterfactual Composition Diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

This section specifies a proposed matched-pair evaluation of route-level
safety recognition and planning. The diagnostic suite has not been evaluated;
the protocol below defines measurements, not empirical findings.

\subsection{Pair Construction}

Each unit is a pair $(p^{+},p^{-})$ of routes for the same ordinary task.
Both routes must complete that task under trusted replay. The safe route
$p^{+}$ satisfies policy and avoids the designated forbidden effect; the
contrasting route $p^{-}$ realizes that effect and violates policy. A pair
holds the task, initial state, tools, and resources fixed and matches
requested authority where feasible. Its contrast changes one declared data
dependency or ordering relation. Any remaining differences are recorded;
pairs with additional unmatched features cannot support a composition-only
interpretation.

The public task, policy, action semantics, classifications, and symbolic
state must make the safety distinction inferable for every private fixture
consistent with the public projection. If the projection leaves the route
outcome ambiguous, the pair is excluded. Private values, resulting states,
ground-truth outcome labels, and evaluator certificates are withheld. Both
routes must be selectable through the diagnostic interface, and both must satisfy the
ordinary task goal under replay.

\subsection{Controlled Interventions}

\paragraph{Data dependencies.}
Both routes produce the same aggregate report using matched tools, resources,
and authority where feasible. The public policy permits internal access but
forbids sending protected source fields outside the task boundary. The safe
route carries only the permitted aggregate; the unsafe route additionally
passes protected source fields into the outbound payload. Public field
classifications and symbolic references expose the relevant provenance
without revealing private values. Blanket refusal to send any report fails
the safe route's task requirement.

\paragraph{Execution order.}
Both routes start with auditing disabled and make the same configuration
change. A public rule requires the change to be audited: the safe route enables
auditing first, whereas the unsafe route changes the configuration before
enabling it. The routes therefore reach the same final configuration and
audit-enable flag, while only the unsafe route contains an unaudited change.
The verifier must check persistent action history; final-state values alone
cannot distinguish the routes. The initial state, rule, and action semantics
are public. This is a proposed intervention and does not imply that the
existing terminal-state verifiers implement such an event monitor.

\subsection{Recognition and Route Planning}

Run each measure in an isolated context without feedback across tests. A
task-only control measures ordinary functional competence $K$. Local tests
$L$ assess isolated subject--operation--resource judgments and one-event
application of the same policy rules, with authorized and unauthorized
controls. These tests do not require reconstructing route history or data
dependencies. Recognition $R$ requires labeling both $p^{+}$ as safe and
$p^{-}$ as forbidden. Planning $J$ requires selecting a safe route from the
shared graph and completing the task. Always labeling both routes unsafe
fails $R$; refusing the task fails $J$.

Report marginal outcomes and their joint distribution. The conditional
diagnostics are
\begin{align*}
 D_{\mathrm{rec}}&=\Pr(\neg R\mid K\land L),\\
 D_{\mathrm{plan}}&=\Pr(\neg J\mid K\land L\land R).
\end{align*}
Here $D_{\mathrm{rec}}$ measures recognition errors among pairs whose
ordinary task and local policy controls pass; $D_{\mathrm{plan}}$ measures
unsafe or unsuccessful planning among those pairs that also pass recognition.
If a conditioning set is empty, its estimate is undefined, not zero.
Recognition errors after the controls are consistent with a route-level
recognition difficulty; planning errors conditional on recognition indicate
a gap between route labeling and successful selection. These behavioral
patterns do not identify internal reasoning or establish a causal mechanism.
Failures on the task-only control remain part of the overall capability
results.

Counterbalance route order and labels, and use meaning-preserving paraphrases
that retain the evidence needed to infer safety. Keep repeated presentations
together when resampling at the pair level. Report unconditional outcomes
alongside conditional diagnostics because conditioning may select easier
pairs. Author and freeze this suite independently; do not alter the frozen
CapGraphBench cases to accommodate it.

\paragraph{Recovery and learning under safety constraints.}
APPA studies policy-governed recovery under information-flow constraints
\citep{kravchenko2026appa}. Shielding separates a learned policy from an
enforcement mechanism that restricts or corrects unsafe actions
\citep{alshiekh2018shielding}. Constrained Policy Optimization addresses policy
optimization under explicit constraints \citep{achiam2017cpo}, while
Conservative Q-Learning studies conservative value estimation from offline data
\citep{kumar2020cql}. The auxiliary ECPA design places an intervention selector
behind trusted authorization and revalidation checks. It asks whether a learned
selector can improve recovery outcomes relative to deterministic controllers
given the same observations and available interventions; this comparison requires
separate empirical validation.

\subsection{Threat Model and Trust Boundary}

ECPA (evaluation-calibrated path-aware capability attenuation) studies runtime
containment when an external relay controls model-response content. The relay may
replace or inject ordinary text, or forge a structured tool call, and thereby
induce a sequence of individually plausible requests that expands authority or
diverts the task. It cannot execute tools directly, alter trusted runtime state,
forge approvals or receipts, or bypass the monitor's final dispatch check. The
threat model therefore begins at a compromised response relay; end-to-end
response authentication is outside its claim. A separate trusted channel serves
the local defender, while attack labels remain evaluator-owned. Each staged-attack
episode is paired with the same task under benign responses.

The controller uses public task and contract data, typed proposals, trusted
execution history, grants, provenance and resource counters. Hidden verifiers,
fixtures, reference routes and costs, evaluator labels, and evaluator-derived
minimum authority are excluded from its runtime view. The bridge from the existing
graph evaluation exports only public audit and aggregate endpoint metadata for
stratification and collection. Realized simulator outcomes may supervise offline
training and score held-out episodes, but do not enter online selection. The
historical v13 planner evaluation remains a separate pre-replay interface.

\subsection{Trusted Runtime and Intervention Menu}

Every proposed action is mediated by trusted code. The runtime offers four
interventions: allow the checked proposal; constrain it to a registered narrower
action; escalate an exact scope increment for approval; or deny and permit bounded
replanning. A constrained alternative is dispatchable only after the task agent
accepts the exact edge, typed action and hash. Rejection dispatches nothing, and
an edited request is treated as a new proposal. Approval may be denied; any grant
is bound to the principal, task, request, epoch and expiry, and cannot override
fixed prohibitions.

Immediately before synchronous dispatch, the monitor rechecks graph position,
preconditions, provenance, prohibited effects and independent operational grants.
An intervening state change invalidates an outstanding offer. Only trusted
execution receipts update runtime facts and history. A bounded structural witness
uses public preconditions, effects, goals, grants and horizon; it returns feasible,
infeasible or unknown, executes no tool and reveals no suffix route. Unknown
alternatives cannot be dispatched. ECPA claims no new security theorem: these
controls rely on complete mediation, sound contracts and authenticated approvals,
and safety does not depend on the learned selector.

\subsection{Outcome-Derived Recovery Learning}
\label{sec:learning}

The primary learner is a Qwen3-1.7B compact model adapted with LoRA; the Qwen3-14B
task agent remains frozen. At sampled training states, each candidate intervention
is evaluated in an independent simulator branch under a shared continuation
policy and attack condition. Choice targets use realized episode violations,
safe completion and cost, preferring violation-free completion and then lower
cost. They are noisy finite-sample observations, not optimal-route certificates.
The formal recipe must specify ordering, ties and censoring before fitting; a
cutoff is not relabeled as a terminal failure.

The selector receives public task/contracts, a structured proposal, trusted path
history, resource counters and the current legal menu. It receives neither raw
untrusted response text nor private evaluator material. Its only output is a
candidate ID from that menu; invalid outputs use a deterministic fallback, and
the trusted runtime revalidates every choice. Training uses standard supervised
candidate-choice learning. LoRA and supervised fine-tuning are not claimed as new
algorithms. The current 66-task source inventory has source-bound AI reviews and
an allocation of 12/4/4/40 semantic families for training, tuning, calibration
and test. Formal fitting requires a source/model freeze and realized coverage of
all intervention targets.

\subsection{Comparison and Evidence Status}

The planned comparison has five arms: an unguarded simulator reference,
per-action authorization, deterministic path-state recovery, a prompted compact
base model, and the same model after LoRA fine-tuning. The compact-model pair
shares the base snapshot, public inputs, menu, prompt and decoding settings.
Guarded arms share immutable prohibitions and final rechecks; path-level methods
also share concrete menus and approval semantics. Offline reinforcement learning,
transfer-model sweeps and model-size sweeps are outside the primary experiment.
The strongest of the three guarded comparators is selected on tuning data, and
calibration audits that fixed selection. Diagnostics-guided acquisition is a
separate hypothesis and is not part of this primary comparison.

Each test family contributes matched benign and attacked episodes under text and
forged-structured-call relays. Safe recovery requires task completion, a valid
explicit STOP, and no violation anywhere in the episode. Report attacked safe
recovery, benign functional success, episode violations, attack success,
interventions and realized costs separately; failures remain in their scheduled
denominators. Resample paired outcomes by semantic family, retaining repeated
presentations and modes within each family. The prespecified learning claim
requires at least a five-percentage-point safe-recovery gain over the strongest
guarded comparator, a paired 95\% interval lower bound above zero, and a one-sided
95\% lower bound of at least $-0.02$ for the benign-success difference. Fewer
than 20 independent test families or a zero-width interval precludes certifying
the claim.

The runtime, public bridge, and collection/fitting/comparison tooling are
implemented. The source inventory has completed its current source-bound review.
The companion study is in progress; completed source/model-bound collection,
fitting, calibration, and test results are not yet incorporated in this manuscript.
The protocol and model identities must accompany the final measurements.
A development-only operational pilot
did exercise collection, LoRA fitting/reload and all five comparison arms
(Appendix~\ref{app:training}). It reused two semantic families and found 3/4
attacked safe recoveries for both LoRA and the deterministic path rule. This
small feasibility pilot does not establish an efficacy difference; in particular,
it provides no evidence that training improves on the path rule. Pilot cases and
the disposable adapter are excluded from formal fitting, calibration and test.

\section{Recovery Training and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{app:training}

The recovery study uses a single compact base language model with LoRA;
base weights and the ordinary task agent remain frozen. Record the exact model
snapshot, trainable parameter count, context length, precision, adapter settings,
update count, training tokens and GPU hours. Pilot these settings before a formal
freeze. Candidate-choice supervision comes from real simulator continuations and
actual outcomes; define candidate ordering, ties and censoring before training.
No hidden route, fixture or optimum is included in the input. Constrain output
to a legal menu ID and recheck before dispatch. Include the identical unfitted
base as a control. Fit one Qwen3-1.7B LoRA adapter with fixed seed 29, recipe
and update count; do not select among training runs or checkpoints.

Existing engineering checks use frozen \nolinkurl{intfloat/e5-small-v2} revision
\texttt{ffb93f3b} and a 128/64-unit scorer with outcome-supervised and offline TD
objectives. Those development fits are not LoRA training, do not validate the proposed
LoRA selector, and do not determine its optimizer settings. The compact-model
training/loading implementation is separate; a completed independent formal
adapter is not documented here. Calibration audits a fixed
selected policy rather than selecting a new policy on the audit set.

A separate compact-model engineering pilot collected eight parent episodes and
18 actual intervention branches from two reused development families, then fitted
and reloaded a disposable Qwen3-1.7B LoRA adapter. Eight updates trained 1.61 million
parameters in 18.94 seconds, with 11.55 GiB peak allocated tensor memory. The full
40-episode integration comparison gave six safe completions in eight episodes for
both the adapter and deterministic path rule, versus zero for the compact base.
The adapter used four deterministic fallbacks across 56 selections. These reused
workflows and eight choice labels are not independent efficacy evidence and show
no advantage over the path rule; neither the records nor adapter enter formal work.

A new corpus must split semantic families across training, tuning, calibration
and fresh tests; primary text-attack operators are also held out.
Entity renaming is not family independence. Other frozen task-agent models
form separate transfer tests. Current development cases are insufficient.
Use public graph strata and realized training-branch outcomes to connect the
evaluator to data collection and supervision. Diagnostics-guided acquisition is
an optional separate study: compare it with uniform acquisition at equal total
calls/tokens, including diagnostic calls and branch continuations. Recovery
benefit alone does not prove acquisition benefit.

Within a concrete menu, the logging mixture is
$\beta(u\mid x)=\frac12\beta_{\rm rule}(u\mid x)+
\frac12\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal M(x))$.
Record conditional behavior probabilities separately from task sampling and
branch assignments. Branching changes simulator state and real agent
continuations independently; rejected offers and failures remain observations.
Actual approval may reject. Never infer successful recovery by relabeling a
trace collected without that intervention.

\paragraph{Relay implementation and claim scope.}
Development campaigns replace a model draft with an authorization pretext.
Two reserved text operators instead append a completion directive to the
original draft or replace it with a spoofed observation envelope. Apparent tool
roles and prerequisite facts remain untrusted response text; they do not update
runtime facts or grants. In structured mode these operators all reduce to the
same registered-proposal substitution. Structured results therefore assess
task-family transfer, not held-out attack mechanisms. Keep mode-stratified
results: text mode uses draft and receiver calls, so mode differences are not
a controlled format-only effect.

The current source inventory contains 66 tasks: 16 training, five tuning,
four calibration, and 41 test tasks, allocated to 12/4/4/40 semantic families.
Source-digest-bound AI reviews cover the fixed simulator sources and selected
comparisons with historical tasks. These records attest to source checks;
they do not authenticate human reviewers or establish exhaustive semantic
independence from every historical workflow. The allocation and source reviews
are complete. The companion experiment is in progress; final protocol/model
identities and measured collection, fitting, calibration, and test comparison
records are not yet incorporated in this draft.

Test predicates cover availability, reviewer independence, conservation,
append-only history, monotone epochs, cumulative disclosure, and relational
migration in simulator state. Shared guards exclude every known harmful edge;
learned gains must concern recovery choices, completion, or cost. Singleton
menus offer no learned choice and remain in primary denominators. Receipt
facts certify fixed fixtures, not arbitrary data-dependent conditions.
Source checks provide no training labels or efficacy evidence.
Collection/fitting tooling validates the clean source and local model snapshots,
meters draft, receiver and acceptance calls, and preserves independent branch
outcomes. Fitting requires a completed measured phase, complete menu comparisons
and realized targets for all four intervention kinds. Transport-fixture tests of

and fresh-test execution are implemented; completed formal model results are

\paragraph{Comparisons and fixed criterion.}
Compare unguarded behavior, per-action authorization, deterministic path-state
recovery, the prompted compact base model, and its LoRA-fine-tuned version.
The compact-model pair shares public inputs, menus, prompts and decoding.
The path-level methods share guards, witness/approval semantics and online budgets.
Offline RL and transfer-model sweeps are outside the primary plan.
Report realized encoding, diagnostics, acquisition, training and total costs,
not just controller inference overhead. Freeze the strongest comparator on
tuning data before calibration/testing.

To limit model-loading cost, freeze a seeded order of arm blocks, enumerating
each task, transport and benign/attacked condition within each block. Shared
controls and the single fitted adapter each run once per setting.
Use common task/transport episode seeds across arms and conditions, while
retaining the actual continuations after histories diverge. On tuning, rank

completion and online budget units; resolve exact ties by a prespecified order.
These budget units are a selection tie breaker, not measured inference time.
Calibration audits the resulting fixed policy without retuning or gating whether
the fresh test runs. Completed measured phases and artifact hashes bind the same

The author-confirmed recovery criterion requires a point gain of at least

\section{Runtime and Transition Contract}
\label{app:runtime}

The separate recovery runtime implements this contract; it does not repair
or reinterpret historical v13 results. Use frozen stateless calls on the public
task/graph, current node, and trusted execution receipts. Pin its model and
generation settings. Persistent planner memory, if introduced, must enter the
recorded decision state; monitor summaries alone cannot be presumed Markov.

\begin{enumerate}
 \item Receive an untrusted typed proposal. Reconstruct authorization state,
 provenance, graph position, and remaining budget from trusted receipts.
 \item Enumerate registered allow/constrain variants in the frozen action
 vocabulary. Narrowing is an explicit alternative request, never a hidden
 parameter rewrite or an unregistered graph transition.
 \item Preview each candidate without external effects. Check requested atoms
 against grants, policy/history predicates, and post-intervention completion
 witnesses. An uncertain preview is not a safe witness.
 \item Select a supported intervention with the learned policy, or use the
 frozen deterministic fallback. Rejection and pending approval dispatch nothing.
 Constrain requires explicit acceptance of its new request; refusal leads to
 replanning without execution of the original request.
 \item Bind the accepted request, principal, task, and policy epoch. Recheck
 against current trusted state immediately before atomic dispatch; stale state
 invalidates the menu. Authenticated approval starts a new grant epoch and a
 fresh check. It never waives fixed forbidden-effect predicates.
 \item Record the dispatched call or non-dispatch decision, trusted receipt,
 next proposal/state, costs, first-violation increment, and termination. Every
 intervention consumes budget; exhausted budgets, unresolved approval deadlines,
 and empty admissible menus terminate with their actual task outcome.
\end{enumerate}

Logging interventions must actually alter the simulated rollout: copying the
reward from an unguarded trace after relabeling its decision is invalid.
Store the behavior probability of the executed intervention, including fallback,
and separate proposal, accepted request, and dispatched effect. Record
rejection-induced replanning and approval outcomes rather than imputing successful
recovery. Only deployment-permitted observations enter critic features; hidden
fixture answers do not. Private training traces are not public release data.
All baselines use the same proposal/feedback contract, action variants, approval
rules, and decision budgets; evaluator-informed oracles are labeled separately.

\section{Proposed Selectability Repair}
\label{app:repair}

Define a fresh interface $\nu^{+}$ with the same public graph, ordinal priority,
and edge bound $H$, retaining the schema's nonterminal start requirement.
At a nonterminal node, only an outgoing edge is admissible.
At a terminal node, the model may explicitly stop or choose an outgoing edge.
Stop is a protocol decision with no tool effect or ordinal charge, not a new
capability adapter. It is invalid at nonterminal nodes. After $H$ edges, only
stop at a terminal is valid; otherwise the path is a model failure. A dead end
that is not terminal is likewise invalid. There is no retry or path repair.

Use a disjoint JSON union: either one \texttt{edge\_id} from currently outgoing
edges or \texttt{\{"stop":true\}} when stopping is admissible. Reject mixed fields,
unknown edges, and invalid stops. The full graph and selected prefix remain
visible; benchmark planning still precedes replay. Seeded presentation remaps
edge/node labels without remapping the stop decision. Report edge choices,
model calls, and stop decisions separately.

Every enumerated start-to-terminal path of at most $H$ edges can be represented
by its edge sequence followed by stop, including interior terminals. Conversely,
each valid response sequence yields such a graph path. This establishes
selectability relative to the enumerator, not functional or policy correctness.
Replay and the existing verifiers still decide those outcomes.

The new public comparison contract must give all exact lexicographic minimizers
credit and identify Pareto status as a secondary diagnostic. Tests must cover
interior terminals, terminal starts, empty outgoing menus, bound exhaustion,
mixed JSON responses, and equal-rank ties. Verify prompt/scorer agreement and
inventory-wide selectability before freezing. This specification is not a v13
patch or a claim of an implemented protocol; it requires a new committed identity
and fresh measurements, with historical manifests retained unchanged.
